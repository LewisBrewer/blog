Taua opens in January 1810 in the north, with news of the Boyd burned at Whangaroa the month before. Ngāpuhi hapū start most of what happens in the game's first twenty years, so this series sets out who they were in 1810 and who led them. This post covers Ngāpuhi as a whole, after that I will start to cover individual hapu and rangitira who would be interesting start positions.

Each post has three parts: the hapū and its history up to 1810, the rangatira and a short look at what came after, and designer's notes on the game system their playthrough leans on most. The kōrero comes from published sources: the Dictionary of New Zealand Biography, Te Ara, Angela Ballara's Taua (2003), the Waitangi Tribunal's Te Paparahi o Te Raki reports, and the whakapapa files I've built from those and other works. I flag the places where hapū accounts differ. If your whānau or hapū holds a different account, please comment below.

Hapū/Ngāpuhi

Ngāpuhi trace their origins to Hokianga. Kupe named the harbour, and Nukutawhiti and Ruanui came later on Ngātokimatawhaorua and settled there. The founding ancestor, Rāhiri, descends from Kupe, Nukutawhiti and Puhi. Rāhiri's two sons fought over land, and he made peace between them by flying a kite over the territory. The place it came down marked the boundary between Hokianga and Taumārere.

Over the generations Rāhiri's descendants moved east, from Hokianga and Kaikohe across the Taiāmai plains and into the Bay of Islands, marrying into some of the groups already there and driving out others. Ngāpuhi hapū pushed Ngāti Pou off Taiāmai in the late 1700s. The Ngāti Pou saying "Ka kata ngā pūriri o Taiamai", the pūriri of Taiāmai are laughing, gave Sissons, Wi Hongi and Hohepa the title of their political history of the inland Bay. Over time the name Ngāpuhi came to cover the hapū of Hokianga and the Bay of Islands, and Rāwiri Taonui's Te Ara entry sets the rohe from Hokianga to the Bay and south to Maunganui Bluff and Whangārei.

In 1810 Ngāpuhi had no single leader. The Tribunal's words are "each hapū was autonomous and exercised power over its own territories". A hapū held its own lands, gardens, fishing grounds and pā under rangatira whose standing came from whakapapa and grew or shrank with what they did. Hapū also split by place. The game carries Ngāti Manu ki Paihia, Ngāti Manu ki Ōpua and Ngāti Manu ki Kāretu as separate groups that share a name and most of their politics, each with its own seat and rangatira.

The Bay/1810

Historians describe the Bay of Islands hapū of this period as two loose groupings. A northern one sat around Kerikeri, Te Puna and Waimate, with Ngāti Tautahi, Ngāti Rēhia, Te Hikutū and Ngāi Tāwake. A southern one sat around Taumārere, Kawakawa and Kororāreka, with Ngāti Manu, Ngāti Hine and their neighbours. Hokianga hapū such as Ngāti Hao and Te Māhurehure kept to their own politics on the west coast, as did Whangaroa to the north. In the eastern Bay, Ngāre Raumati stood apart from both groupings and fought each of them at times.

Hapū married across these groupings, changed sides and fought inside them. In 1830 northern and southern hapū fought the Girls' War on the beach at Kororāreka, and by the end of the 1820s Ngāre Raumati had abandoned their stronghold at Kahuwera.

Contact/1810

By 1810 the Bay of Islands was one of the main anchorages in the southwest Pacific. Whalers and Sydney traders put in for water, timber, pork and potatoes, and paid in iron, cloth and the odd musket. Te Pahi of Te Hikutū, at Te Puna, had stayed with Governor King in Sydney in 1805 and 1806. Hapū with potatoes and pigs had already changed how they spent their labour.

At Moremonui, in 1807 or 1808, Ngāti Whātua caught a Ngāpuhi taua while its musketeers reloaded and killed many of its leaders. Through the 1810s and 1820s Ngāpuhi hapū armed in numbers and carried the guns south.

In December 1809 the Boyd was loading kauri spars at Whangaroa. On the voyage over, the captain had Te Ara, a young rangatira working his passage home, flogged and denied food. His kin took utu and killed most of the people on board. European whalers blamed Te Pahi, although the Whangaroa people said he had no part in it, and destroyed his pā at Te Puna with heavy loss of life. Te Pahi died of wounds later in 1810, in the fighting that followed. Ships stayed away from the north for several years, and Marsden's mission was delayed until 1814.

Rangatira/The series

Some of these leaders held power in 1810. Others were children who grew into it.

· Hongi Hika, Ngāti Tautahi and Te Uri o Hua: war leader after Moremonui, inland from Kerikeri, and the man who armed the north.

· Te Pahi, Ruatara and Waikato, Te Hikutū: the Te Puna and Rangihoua rangatira at the centre of the Boyd's aftermath, the mission's arrival and the first journeys to Sydney and England.

· Patuone and Tāmati Wāka Nene, Ngāti Hao: Hokianga brothers who became go-betweens with Pākehā and the Crown.

· Pōmare I and Pōmare II, Ngā Uri o Pōmare and Ngāti Manu: the southern grouping's war leader through the 1810s and 1820s, and the nephew who followed him.

· Te Morenga, Ngāti Rangi: the Taiāmai rangatira who led his own expeditions south alongside Hongi's.

· Hōne Heke Pōkai, Ngāti Rāhiri and Matarahurahu: a small child in 1810, so his post looks at his people and the man he became.

· Te Whareumu and Kiwikiwi, Ngāti Manu: rangatira of Kororāreka, the beach that became the busiest anchorage in the country.

· Tītore, Ngāi Tāwake: a northern war leader until his death in 1837.

· Korokoro, Ngāre Raumati: the eastern Bay rangatira outside the Ngāpuhi groupings.

· Te Ruki Kawiti, Ngāti Hine: the rangatira inland of Kawakawa who built Ruapekapeka.

Playthrough/Designer's notes

I don't run Ngāpuhi as one faction. Each hapū on that map runs on the same systems as the player's hapū, with its own rangatira, stores, utu ledger and kin ties. I give each seat a grouping for 1810, and it sets where the hapū starts with its neighbours. Kin closeness from the whakapapa data then decides how disputes between them play out, so a northern hapū and a southern hapū with close kin ties handle a quarrel as kin. The groupings shift as marriages, deaths and grievances build up, and the Girls' War can happen, or not, without a script.

Out of 55 Ngāpuhi seats, 12 sit in the northern grouping, 13 in the southern, 7 in Hokianga, 2 in Whangaroa and 3 with Ngāre Raumati.

The north holds the deepest data in the project. Mission journals, Native Land Court minutes and Tribunal evidence cover the Bay of Islands and Whangaroa closer than anywhere else in the period, so the roster there names people in most hapū, with dated deaths and recorded grievances behind them. I also whakapapa from the north, so through personal connections I’ve been able to build out this knowledge further.

Each post in the series centres on the system its playthrough leans on most:

· Hongi Hika: the musket trade, and the utu bill each expedition runs up.

· Te Hikutū: mission contact, the Boyd's fallout and travel overseas.

· Patuone and Nene: peace-making, and dealing with the Crown.

· Pōmare: control of anchorages, and the trade rivalry with the northern grouping.

· Te Morenga: haerenga taua, and the utu that sends them out.

· Hōne Heke: the Treaty, and resistance.

· Te Whareumu and Kiwikiwi: Kororāreka and Pākehā contact on the beach.

· Tītore: alliances and the Girls' War.

· Korokoro: keeping a hapū alive under pressure.

· Kawiti: pā engineering.
