The Whenua post covered the land itself: forest, swamp, fern, rivers and the seasons. This post covers the human created elements on the map. Pā and kāinga, gardens and food places, tracks and waka routes, and the first footholds Pākehā made on the coast.

What was there in 1810

At current the settlement table holds about 1,360 named places across both islands. Of those, 438 are pā, 301 are kāinga, 24 are kāinga with built defences, and 115 are anchorages. The rest are landmarks, resource places and sites unbuilt as of 1810.

For January 1810 the game seats about 670 hapū holdings in the North Island, with close to 125,000 people between them. Te Tai Tokerau holds about 200 of the settled places on its own, the further south you go the thinner the map gets. Part of that reflects where people lived, with population dropping as you move further south, but a lot is due to not having properly explored and mapped the data for southern regions currently.

Pā and kāinga

Each place carries a type and a few physical numbers. The type is pā, kāinga, or kāinga with defences. Natural defence runs from 0 to 5 and measures how defensible is the ground that the structure is built on : a ridge spur, a headland, cliffs, swamp on three sides. Built defence runs from 0 to 3 and measures what additions are made: palisades, ditches, banks, fighting stages. A size tier runs from tiny to huge, and a garden score says how much cultivable land sits close by.

The hardest question for every pā is whether anyone lived in it in 1810. The LINZ gazetteer names and locates hundreds of pā, but it gives no dates. A pā on a ridge might have stood empty for two hundred years by 1810, or its builders might raise it twenty years later. My rule is that an undated pā claims nothing. I record it as an empty site owned by the hapū whose land it sits on, with no population, and I link it to that hapū's nearest seat as a refuge. A source has to put people in a pā before the game does.

Ruapekapeka shows why the rule matters. Kawiti built it in 1845, so in 1810 it is an empty site in Ngāti Hine country. Ōhaeawai is a Ngāti Rangi kāinga with no pā in 1810. The famous forms of both arrive with the war.

As I find more sources, I’ll keep fixing mistakes as well as filling in gaps. Crosby names Matara at lower Waihou in Hokianga as the Ngāti Pou pā of Te Tihi, which Hongi took in 1813. My data had seated Te Tihi's people at Mataraua, an inland place with a similar name. Matara now holds them.

Holdings, seats and rangatira

A place is ground. The people on it sit in a second table, structured so that one place can hold several groups at once. A controlling hapū holds the place. Resident groups live there under its mana. Some rows record a hapū that held a place earlier and had left it by 1810. Each group carries a population, a flag for whether this is the hapū's principal seat, and the rangatira who holds it, linked to the whakapapa data.

Every change to these tables goes into a decisions log, now past a hundred rulings. Some are small, such as raising Te Morenga's seat at Taiāmai from 35 people to 400 so he can fill the waka taua he is recorded to lead in the 1820s. Others move people. Tītore lived at Whakataha pā and Waimate in 1810. He took the Kororāreka beach twenty years later, so his household moved inland.

Some seats stay empty on purpose. Where no source and no person in the whakapapa data fits, the seat waits. I'd rather show an empty seat than a wrong name.

Gardens and food places

Gardens sit at three levels in the data. Each kāinga has a garden score. Over 1,000 resource sites mark the ground that fed people:

· forests for timber

· mahinga kai

· fishing grounds and eel weirs

· harakeke swamps

· fertile river flats and kūmara grounds

· hot springs

· stone sources

Beneath those, the land is cut into about 14,300 parcels, small blocks of ground with a customary hapū, a garden share, and shares of each resource.

The parcels grow out from each 1810 holding across the terrain. Borders follow ridges, swamps and big rivers. Parcels run small near gardens and large in the bush. Each one records which hapū held it by custom, and later in the game it records any deal that changed its hands.

Tracks and waka routes

The data holds 572 routes. 404 are walking tracks, 90 are sea lanes along the coast, 71 are river routes, and 7 are portages where waka crossed the land between waters. I traced most of the walking tracks from a map of major tracks and water routes. The rivers come from LINZ river outlines, thinned to single channels, and I placed the rest by hand in the place editor.

Two kinds of link sit on top of the routes. Each kāinga with no defences links to a pā where its people could take refuge, and there are now 129 of those links. Each anchorage links to the settlements it served, 264 links in all. Together they say where a hapū runs when a taua comes, and where its goods reach the sea.

Pākehā footholds

In 1810 the Pākehā presence is ships at anchor. The data marks 115 anchorages with their shelter, depth and bar hazard, and flags three where ships traded in 1810. Shore footholds arrive later, each with a rough start year:

· 1814: the Church Missionary Society at Rangihoua

· 1819: Kerikeri

· 1823: Paihia, and the Wesleyans at Wesleydale

· 1825: Sydney traders at Kororāreka

· 1826: the Deptford shipyard at Hōreke

· 1827: the Wesleyans at Mangungu

· 1830: Te Waimate

· 1839: the Catholic mission at Kororāreka

Land deals follow the footholds. The first in my data is the mission ground at Oihi below Rangihoua, which Te Hikutū granted in February 1815 for twelve axes. Each deal carries a footprint, a grantor hapū and a recipient, and the parcels it covers change hands in play.

How a place gets in

Each place records where its position came from, so I can judge how far to trust it. About 500 sit on a LINZ gazetteer entry, either a named pā or a locality I matched to the source. About 160 come from Crosby's The Musket Wars, about 150 from W. A. Taylor's Lore and History of the South Island Maori, and about 80 I tweaked by hand. About 100 are still estimates. Anne Salmond's maps of Bay of Islands chiefs in Between Worlds tie hapū and rangatira to pā for the years around 1810. The Tribunal's district reports, the Old Land Claims files and Turton's deeds fill in who held what.
