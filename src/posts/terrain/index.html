The campaign map is where most of Taua gets played, so the ground has a lot to do. It has to read clearly from far above, sit exactly under the base map, the places and the tracks, and still look like Te Tai Tokerau. This post follows the terrain for the core slice through eleven passes, from raw contour lines to a map with rivers, bush, paths, settlements and rock that holds up in Unity. The core slice runs from the Hokianga to Whangārei and takes in the Bay of Islands and Whangaroa. Prior to modelling and adjusting by hand I wanted to see how much I could get in by importing LINZ data and my own maps and adjusting/tweaking them.

Starting from the contours

The ground starts as the LINZ Topo50 contours with the standard warp applied to enlarge high traffic areas. They're burned onto a 20 m grid and filled into a surface. The core slice comes out as 24 chunks of about 41 km each, 2,049 samples a side.

The height needed exaggerating. At true scale Northland is almost flat from a campaign camera. At 3× the ranges barely stood out, and at 7× the hills turned spiky, 5× was a good compromise.

Readable Terrain

Real Northland country is a tangle of small, steep, dissected hills. Exaggerated five times, the filled contours read like noise. Most of the early passes were about taking detail away.

  • Valley floors: the first 45 m above the local valley level is squashed, so floors go near-flat and hills rise out of them.
  • High ground: heights from 80 m to 600 m get progressively more lift, up to 35%, so ranges stand out from the lowland.
  • Erosion: droplet erosion at 80 m carves a few broad gullies rather than many fine ones, and drops most of its sediment so the valleys stay flat.
  • Generalised hills: the land is smoothed at a 650 m scale, keeping only a trace of the fine detail. This is what finally killed the comb streaks.
  • Crests: convex ridges and peaks above 200 m are sharpened, for broad, faceted ridgelines like a painted campaign map.

The coast

Coasts matter in a game about waka taua, so the map draws two kinds. Cliffs I paint by hand in the Whenua editor become sheer drops at least 55 m high, with deep water at their feet. The ground behind a cliff eases down inland, so a cliff reads as the edge of raised country and not a wall in front of a plain. Wherever the coast meets LINZ sand, it becomes a landing beach instead: a low sand apron 220 m wide over a shallow shelf. Low, wide and shallow against tall, sheer and deep, so a player can see at a glance where a fleet can come ashore. Harbours are found by their narrow mouths and kept as tidal flats with a deeper channel.

Rivers

Rivers took three goes. The first matched the base map's own ranking of the LINZ rivers, which kept the two in step but left some rivers ending in the middle of nowhere. The second extended every river to the sea, this proved inexact and cluttered, so in the end i had them rebuilt from LINZ drainage maps: the terrain works out how much land drains through every cell, with the mapped rivers burned in so the channels follow them.

A river draining 150 km² or more is a major river. There are 37 of them in the slice, 662 km in all, and only those are carved, 150 to 450 m wide by catchment, sitting just below a flat floodplain. The 325 smaller rivers are drawn as thin lines and left uncarved, which keeps the land between them readable. Major rivers block armies except at fords, and there are 71 fords, placed wherever a historical track or a settlement link crosses, since that's where people crossed. I’ll still go in by hand afterwards to clean these up but it’s a good start.

Bush

The forest uses my own tree models as detailed in the previous post, scattered by density maps built from the Whenua state grid: forested, mosaic, scrub, fernland and swamp. Hero trees mark the landscape types: kauri on the ridges of the northern kauri forest, kahikatea through swamp forest, and pōhutukawa along the coast and over the water.

Scale was the big decision. At real size the trees vanish from a campaign camera, so they're about 14 times life size, which makes a canopy group around 520 m across. Rivers and shorelines would disappear under crowns that big, so the canopy fades out near open water and the forest leaves room for them. The northland slice uses about 233,000 trees.

All of that has to run on a laptop, so trees and terrain switch detail with distance. Each tree model has two reduced versions, and the terrain has three tiers: 20 m close in, 80 m in the middle distance and 320 m beyond 40 km.

Ground, paths and settlements

After a few more revisions, I was ready to retexture the land. The ground is now nine painted, tiling textures (fern and grass, forest floor, scrub, rock, cliff, sand, wetland, earth and marsh water) blended by splat maps.

The tracks come from the Place Editor. Each one was checked against the ground first: redrawing tracks that crossed mountain ranges through a saddle or along a valley, and rerouting tracks that go across a harbour to go round it by land. Then every settlement gets a route to the network, and the settlements of each hapū and each iwi are joined to each other. That added 540 new links, which went back into the track data so the game and the terrain share one network. In the tenth pass the paths became pale trodden sand with the trees trimmed back so they read through the bush.

Settlements are grey-boxes for now, sized against the trees: whare puni, whare nui, pātaka, kauta, palisades, fighting stages, waka and gardens.

Seeing them on the finished ground showed the next problem. The kit is one layout rule applied everywhere, and it fails in three ways:

  • Too big for the hilltops. Pā are laid out by place size, not by the ground under them. On a narrow summit the full kit spills down the slopes, with houses stacked on each other and palisades running off the edge. Onewhero's houses sit right on a cliff edge.
  • Too small in the bush. Inland, kāinga such as Mohinui, Maromaku and Motatau almost disappear into the canopy from campaign height. They need larger pieces or wider clearings to read.
  • Too generic for the places that matter. Rangihoua, Whangaroa pā and Kāeo each had their own form, and a ring sized from a number doesn't capture it. Kāeo's ring is far bigger than the valley floor around it.

So settlements will split into two kinds. Most places keep the generated kit, manually positioned and tweaked to fit the ground: a pā on a small summit should shrink to the space the hilltop has. A short list of hero settlements will get custom placements, laid out by hand in Blender against the terrain, then saved as models that replace the generated kit for that place.

Impassable Terrain

Like the Shogun 2 and Three Kingdoms campaign maps, Taua has impassable terrain: the rugged country between the valleys. It's generated from the relief and smoothed into whole blocks, about 17% of the land, and I can repaint it by hand in the Whenua editor. Settlements, tracks and the major rivers are always kept passable, and every settlement was checked to make sure it can reach the open ground of its landmass.

In the tenth pass I wanted open water in the raupō swamps, like the paddy water on the Three Kingdoms map. My first attempt involved using depth maps to carve pools into the ground, but it looked horrible and broke a lot of systems (e.g scattering pohutakawa all over the water edges). The final version paints the water as a ground texture and leaves the terrain alone, while the movement grid still marks the pools as impassable. About a third of each marsh shows open water, with banks between that armies can use.

Mountains

The impassable country needed to look the part. My first attempt raised it into rock massifs, and they came out bulbous, more like boulders than mountains.They were then rebuilt as ridged ranges: gullies follow the real drainage, height climbs at a steep fixed slope away from each gully so knife-edge ridges form on the watersheds, a rough skirt of foothills runs round each range, and pale scree fans spread where the bigger gullies come out.

Rock

The eleventh pass added rock as quick meshes that I can edit later on in blender. There are 19 models: cliff pieces, sea stacks, hilltop tors, range crags, boulders and two landmark forms. Each is a pack of jointed blocks, so the faces stay crisp and read as fractured rock from above. The pipeline places about 6,000 of them: cliff pieces along every painted cliff, facing the sea, sea stacks off some of those cliffs, tors on the summits of the smaller hills, crags on the higher peaks, and boulders on the scree fans. Like the trees, they're scaled to read at campaign zoom rather than to real life, and the forest is kept off them so they show through the bush.

Whangaroa

Rule-based stylising works for most of the country, but some landscapes are their own thing and need to be treated that way.

Whangaroa Harbour is a drowned valley ringed by bush-clad volcanic domes and spires. In Unity it came out almost flat. The same rules that tidied up the rest of Northland had planed its landmarks down: Ōhakiri (St Paul's Rock) to 26 m and Kairara (Duke's Nose) to 59 m.

So Whangaroa became my first test of a landscape zone, an area that keeps its real relief instead of being generalised and flattened. Inside it the ground follows the contours with 35% more height over the local low. St Paul's now rises to 229 m and Kairara to 303 m, with a steep plug under a rock spire. The steep slopes stay in bush rather than bare rock paint, as they are in the real harbour.

Seeing it in Unity

The last problem wasn't in the terrain at all. In Unity the ranges read as one flat, dark tone with no shadows, even though the data had plenty of relief. The cause was the scene lighting. At this scale one Unity unit is 20 m, and URP's default shadow distance is 50 units, so nothing cast a shadow from the campaign camera. The fix sets a low sun in the north-west, a dimmer ambient so shaded slopes go darker than lit ones, and shadows out to 120 km. A distance haze layers the ranges back, and an optional triplanar shader stops the rock texture streaking down steep faces.
